Year-End Office Moves: Labeling Desks, IT and Files

November 20, 2026

← All Posts

A year-end office move looks simple on a calendar: pack Friday, move over the weekend, be open Monday. It only works if every desk, monitor, and file box has a clear home on the other side. If the labels are vague, Monday morning is a scavenger hunt.

TAG-A-ROOM office labels are built for that job. They keep an office move organized from desk to floor, so IT, files, and furniture land in the right rooms instead of in a pile by the elevator.

This post is about labeling a workplace move at the end of the year: desks, IT, and files, plus the boxes that have to be opened first. It is not a packing-services sales page, and it is not about holiday decorations at home. If you also need general box content labels for mixed cartons, those are in the box content labels shop.

Why year-end office moves go sideways without labels

Q4 is when leases end, departments consolidate, and companies try to be in the new space before January. The move itself often happens on a weekend or after hours, with a mix of staff, movers, and sometimes an IT vendor who has never seen the floor plan.

That is a lot of hands on a lot of similar-looking boxes. A "files" box and a "desk 14" box look the same once they're stacked in a truck. A server, a box of personnel files, and a crate of keyboards can sit next to each other in a hallway, and nobody can tell which one has to be plugged in first.

Office labels fix the identification problem before the tape goes on. You write the desk, the department, the room, and what is inside. The people loading the truck, and the people unpacking it, can read the same plan.

Label desks like rooms, not like leftover furniture

In a house, one room gets one color. In an office, one desk is its own small room. If you only label the floor or the conference room, individual desks get mixed, and people spend Monday hunting for their monitors.

A useful desk label usually has:

  • The person's name or desk number
  • The department
  • The destination room or zone in the new office
  • A short contents line if the box is not obvious: "desk drawers," "monitor and dock," "personal items"

Do this before the drawers are dumped into a carton. Once everything is in brown boxes, you will not remember which stack was whose.

If two people share a desk or a bench, label each person's boxes separately. "Marketing, north wall" is better than "office," but "Jordan, Marketing, Desk 12, north wall" is what actually gets the dock and the chair to the same place.

Number the boxes at each desk. "Desk 12, box 2 of 4" tells the movers there are more, and it tells the employee when the set is complete.

IT needs labels that a tech can trust

IT is where a vague label gets expensive. A box marked "electronics" does not tell anyone whether it's a spare mouse or the conference-room codec.

For IT, be more specific than you think you need to be:

  • Device type: laptop, monitor, dock, phone, printer, server equipment
  • Whose it is, or which desk or room it belongs to
  • Whether it has to be set up first thing Monday
  • Cables and small parts in the same box, noted on the label: "dock, power supply, and HDMI in box"

Keep passwords, spare keys, and backup drives out of open boxes, and do not write sensitive information on a label. The label should identify the equipment, not the data inside it.

If a piece cannot be stacked, cannot go on the truck yet, or has to be handled a certain way, that is what alert labels are for. "Open first," "do not load," "fragile," and "caution" flag what matters at a glance, so a mover does not have to guess.

Servers, network closets, and shared printers should be labeled as destinations, not as miscellaneous IT. "Server closet, unpack first" is a different instruction than "IT supplies."

Files: label the contents and the access level you can say out loud

File boxes all look the same. That is why they get opened in the wrong room, or left in a hallway until someone has time to investigate.

On the office label, write:

  • The department
  • The file group, in plain language: "2026 vendor contracts," "HR archive, 2019 to 2022," "active client folders"
  • The destination: a specific cabinet, room, or storage area
  • Whether it is needed in the first week, or can go into storage

Do not put confidential details, account numbers, or personal data on the outside of the box. The label should be useful to the people moving it, and boring to anyone else who walks past.

If a set of files has to be available Monday morning, use an alert label such as "Open first" in addition to the office label. If a box should not go on the first truck, or should not be stacked, say that with "Do not load" or "Caution," rather than hoping the crew notices a sticky note.

Flag the pieces that cannot wait in a stack

Alert labels exist for the exceptions: open first, do not load, fragile, caution. In a year-end office move, those usually belong on:

  • The box with badges, keys, or the door-sign kit
  • Shared equipment the team needs immediately: the main printer, the conference camera, the payment terminal
  • Fragile items: glass desktop accessories, framed items, specialty equipment
  • Anything that should not be loaded until a room is empty or a vendor is on site

The office label still does the everyday work of saying what it is and where it goes. The alert label is the flag. Use both when a box is both important and easy to mishandle.

A simple labeling order for the last week of the year

If the move is booked for a December weekend, this order keeps the labels honest:

  1. Map the new floor. Even a simple list of rooms and desk numbers is enough. Every label should point to a place on that list.
  2. Label desks first, including the chairs and the desk boxes, before people start packing casually.
  3. Label IT next, with enough detail that a technician can unpack without opening every carton.
  4. Label files by department and destination, and mark which boxes are needed in week one.
  5. Add alert labels only where they change how the box should be handled.
  6. Walk the old office once the night before the move. If you still see an unlabeled box, it is not ready.

Do not wait until Friday afternoon to start this. The labels only help if they go on before the tape, and before the weekend crew arrives.

FAQ

What labels should I use for an office move?

Start with office labels for desks, IT, and files. Add alert labels for boxes that should be opened first, not loaded yet, or handled as fragile.

Do I need a different label for each desk?

Yes. Treat each desk like its own small room, with a name or desk number, a department, and a destination in the new office.

What should I write on IT boxes?

The device, the desk or room it belongs to, and whether it has to be set up first. Keep cables with the device, and say so on the label. Do not write passwords or private data on the outside.
